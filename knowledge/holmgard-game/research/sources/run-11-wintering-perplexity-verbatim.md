## Executive summary

Yes: Rus’ participation in eastern trade involved winter residence in eastern Europe, not simply annual summer voyages out of Scandinavia and back. But three different phenomena must be distinguished:

1. Documented winter maintenance of the Rus’ ruling and trading-warrior population in tributary territories.
2. Archaeologically established resident communities with Scandinavian connections at river settlements.
3. Visiting merchants spending a particular winter at an intermediary port—the most difficult category to demonstrate directly.

The strongest contemporary testimony is **Constantine VII**, writing around 950: the Rus’ left Kyiv in November, were maintained among their Slav tributaries throughout winter, returned from April as the Dnieper ice melted, and departed downstream in June. This documents wintering within the Rus’ commercial-political system, but not all wintering in urban merchant lodgings.[1]

**Ibn Fadlan** supplies the clearest description of merchants’ accommodation: Rus’ traders on the Volga occupied substantial riverside wooden houses, ten or twenty people to a house. His account does not, however, say that these particular traders stayed through winter. Nor should his river name *Atil* be confused with a statement that he visited the Khazar capital Itil.[2]

No securely documented, timed itinerary from Birka to Constantinople, Volga Bulgar, or the Caspian emerges from these sources. Experimental voyages provide useful logistical benchmarks—not attested Viking-Age durations. Exact “typical ice-out dates in 920” are likewise unavailable: contemporary seasonal observations can be combined with later hydrology only as explicitly labelled reconstruction.[1][3][4]

### Evidence labels used below

- Documented: explicitly reported by a relevant text, or directly established archaeologically. A textual report remains subject to the author’s knowledge and biases.
- Reconstructed: a plausible interpretation or logistical model, not an explicit historical record.
- Unknown: the evidence reviewed does not establish the requested detail.

## 1. What actually demonstrates overwintering?

### The central methodological distinction

A Scandinavian-style brooch, sword, weight, or dirham establishes neither a Scandinavian individual nor a winter visit by itself. A cemetery and an extensive domestic settlement offer much stronger evidence of resident communities, but even they do not normally identify a particular person as “a Birka merchant who stayed from October to May.”

This matters especially at Gnezdovo: the evidence is not merely for travellers pausing along a route, but for people who made their lives there. Władysław Duczko explicitly interprets it as permanent residence by Norse groups, while acknowledging the difficulty of identifying their buildings separately within the settlement.[5]

### Constantine: direct evidence, but for a different sort of wintering

Chapter 9 of *De administrando imperio* gives this sequence:

- November: the chiefs and Rus’ leave Kyiv for the *poliudia*, the tribute “rounds.”
- Winter: they are maintained in tributary Slav territories.
- From April: they return as the Dnieper ice melts.
- Spring: boats are obtained and fitted out.
- June: the fleet leaves Kyiv and assembles briefly at Vitichev before descending the Dnieper.[1]

“Maintained” is direct evidence for winter provisioning by subject populations. It does not specify whether accommodation took the form of billeting in households, chiefly establishments, purpose-built quarters, or movement between several hosts. Nor should Constantine’s sweeping “all the Russians” be treated as a census of every merchant and resident.[1]

Most importantly, this is an economic cycle: winter was a season for obtaining support and collecting tribute that could feed into summer commerce—not merely an interruption of commerce. Duczko connects tribute collection, storage, and winter subsistence in his reconstruction of the Rus’ system.[5]

### Ibn Fadlan and Ibn Rustah: different evidential strengths

Ibn Fadlan’s 922 account describes traders living ashore rather than permanently sleeping aboard. James Montgomery’s annotated translation records houses holding ten or twenty people, each man with his own couch or sitting-place. Montgomery suggests that local traders might have maintained these buildings for the Rus’, but explicitly presents this as an interpretation, not a statement in the Arabic text.[2]

Ibn Rustah supports the broader picture of Rus’ trade with Bulgars and Khazars and dependence on resources obtained from Slav populations. He does not supply an equivalent of Constantine’s November–April calendar or a clear account of winter merchant lodging. His account is also not an eyewitness narrative comparable to Ibn Fadlan’s encounter.[11][12]

## 2. Overwintering logistics: ships, shelter, and cargo

### Where were ships kept?

| Proposed arrangement | Evidential status |
|---|---|
| Moored in the river during a trading visit | Documented by Ibn Fadlan. It is not explicitly a winter arrangement. [2] |
| Hauled ashore | Documented for a funeral: Ibn Fadlan describes a boat brought ashore and positioned on timber supports. Constantine also describes hauling and portaging boats, demonstrating that river craft could be moved out of the water. Neither passage documents routine winter storage. [13][1] |
| Beached or drawn above the ice zone for winter, with removable equipment stored separately | A reasonable reconstruction, but no site-specific c. 850–970 winter procedure is established by the evidence reviewed. |
| Left afloat and allowed to freeze into the river | Unknown as a normal practice for these trading crews. Ibn Fadlan’s mooring passage cannot establish it. |
| Buried for preservation until spring | Not established. Archaeological boat deposits in these regions are principally funerary contexts, not evidence of seasonal ship caches. [14][15] |
| Kept in purpose-built winter boathouses | Not established for the particular settlements and period under discussion. |

The boats themselves complicate the question. Constantine does not describe one Scandinavian ship making the entire journey unchanged. At Kyiv, the Rus’ bought new *monoxyla* hulls and transferred oars, rowlocks, and other equipment from old craft that they dismantled. At the Dnieper mouth they fitted equipment needed for the coastal voyage. His account therefore documents replacement and reconfiguration within a transport system, rather than an obligatory Birka-to-Byzantium voyage in one hull.[1]

### Who sheltered the traders?

Three arrangements have different levels of support:

1. Communal merchant accommodation: directly described on the Volga by Ibn Fadlan. Ownership, rent, heating arrangements, and winter use are unspecified.[2]
2. Tributary maintenance: directly described by Constantine. The communities supporting the Rus’ were not necessarily voluntary commercial hosts. The text does not provide a lodging tariff.[1]
3. Accommodation through local households, patrons, or resident commercial partners: plausible for visitors at established settlements, but reconstructed for this period unless a specific source says otherwise.

### What the 1269 Novgorod–Lübeck/Gotland material can establish

The later material is valuable as an analogy for organized accommodation, storage, escorts, and dependence on local pilots. It cannot demonstrate that identical institutions existed around 920.

Two cautions are particularly important:

- Some scholarship identifies the relevant 1268/1269 text as a draft; it should not always be treated as a straightforward record of a fully implemented agreement.[16]
- “Winter guest” can mean a merchant arriving in winter. It does not automatically mean someone stranded after a summer voyage. The translated clauses discussed in *Baltic Hospitality* explicitly make this distinction and concern escorts and guarantees of safe travel.[17]

For the later period, the scholarly synthesis discusses merchants lodging aboard ships or in rented accommodation and the development of protected merchant courts. This provides a comparative model, not proof of a tenth-century Hanseatic-style establishment at Ladoga or Novgorod.[17]

### What happened to unsold cargo?

Documented: Ibn Fadlan explicitly says that a trader might have difficulty selling and remain “too many days,” making repeated offerings in hopes of finding a buyer. Waiting with unsold stock was therefore real.[2]

Reconstructed: during a winter stay, portable goods could be retained in occupied buildings under the crew’s or a partner’s supervision; some might be sold locally rather than carried farther. This is a logistical possibility, not an attested inventory system.

Unknown: the normal storage contracts, warehouse charges, insurance-like arrangements, or rules governing cargo left behind while its owner travelled elsewhere.

Enslaved people also formed part of the merchandise. Keeping them through a delayed departure entailed food, shelter, supervision, and coercion, unlike storing an inert bale of goods; Ibn Fadlan documents their presence in the merchant houses but not a winter maintenance budget.[2]

Section sources: *DAI* 9; Montgomery’s translation and commentary; Dubrovin’s study of boat evidence; Jackson, Boestad, and Lukin in *Baltic Hospitality* (2022), chapters 3–5.[1][2][15][17]

## 3. Sailing calendar and total journey duration

### What can actually be dated?

There is no defensible table of measured average ice dates for each waterway in 900–950. The useful distinction is between:

- Contemporary seasonal testimony: Constantine’s April Dnieper thaw and June departure.
- Later hydrological analogues: useful for broad planning windows, not exact medieval dates.
- Reconstructed sailing schedules: dependent on route, current, boat type, portages, market delays, and political conditions.

Ice breakup is also not identical to safe passage. Moving ice, residual lake ice, and different thaw dates along successive waterways could prevent continuous navigation even after one stretch had opened. Modern research treats breakup, ice run, and complete clearance as separate events.[18]

### Approximate seasonal framework

The following is an analogue-based planning table, not a reconstruction of annual averages in the tenth century.

| Waterway | Spring opening: useful broad analogue | Autumn/winter closure: useful broad analogue | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baltic crossing and Gulf of Finland | Generally April into early May for the relevant northern/eastern approaches, varying greatly by locality and winter. The northern Baltic Proper clears earlier than the last northern ice. [4] | Ice begins in the inner Gulf of Finland and northern coastal areas in October–November; the whole Baltic does not normally freeze simultaneously. [4] | Route access depends on coasts and harbours, not merely open water offshore. |
| Lake Ladoga | April–May; published summaries differ according to whether they mean central opening, northern clearance, or any remaining ice. [19][20] | Ice phenomena may begin in November; coastal bays commonly freeze around early December, the central lake later. [19][20] | A river opening did not guarantee a clear lake crossing. |
| Volkhov | Broadly April; modern summaries give early April breakup. [21] | Broadly November; modern summaries give late November freeze-up. [21] | Treat these as lower-confidence analogues, not Viking-Age dates. |
| Upper Dnieper / Gnezdovo corridor | Around April in the later hydrological summary. [22] | Stable ice around early December. [22] | Upper-route timing differs from the lower river. |
| Kyiv and middle Dnieper | April thaw has unusually valuable contemporary support in Constantine. [1] | A cautious planning model would avoid relying on late-autumn navigation; an exact tenth-century freeze date is unknown. | The best historical anchor is the spring thaw, not a precise closing day. |
| Lower Dnieper | Later summary: thaw begins around early March. [22] | Stable ice around late December. [22] | Earlier southern opening does not free a party still upstream. |
| Upper/middle Volga, including the Bulgar approach | Broadly April in later hydrological summaries. [23] | Generally late November. [24][25] | A comparatively short northern river season governs a much longer route. |
| Lower Volga / Itil approach | Broadly March into April; near Astrakhan, breakup begins around mid-March in the cited summary. [23] | Generally December. [24] | The southern section may remain navigable after northern access has closed. |

A practical reconstructed north-to-south boating season would therefore concentrate departures in late spring and major progress in summer, while retaining substantial margins before winter. “May–September” is a conservative planning window for a connected northern itinerary, not a claim that navigation outside it was impossible.

### Attested durations versus experimental durations

| Journey | What is actually attested |
|---|---|
| Birka → Constantinople | No securely timed contemporary through-itinerary identified in the sources reviewed. |
| Birka → Volga Bulgar | No securely timed contemporary through-itinerary identified. |
| Birka → Caspian | No securely timed contemporary through-itinerary identified. |
| Birka → Baghdad | No securely timed contemporary through-itinerary identified; nor should this be imagined as an all-water journey. |
| Central Sweden/Mälaren → Novgorod, experimental vessel | 42 days, according to the National Museum of Denmark’s summary. [3] |
| Novgorod → Dnieper mouth, experimental continuation | 89 days, including more than 250 km of overland dragging. [3] |
| Combined experimental legs | 131 days to the Dnieper mouth—not to Constantinople, and not including a return journey. [3] |

The experiment is informative but not a medieval timetable. Its results depend on the selected route, water conditions, crew, vessel, and portage methods. Conversely, medieval boat replacement, local transport services, or cargo transshipment might change the comparison substantially. Constantine’s account demonstrates that such a journey need not involve one boat throughout.[1][3]

The museum concludes that a central-Sweden–Black-Sea round trip could not fit into one boating season, then makes the stronger claim that every voyage beyond Ladoga required wintering. The first conclusion is a persuasive logistical inference from the experiment; the second is too categorical to apply to every possible short itinerary beyond Ladoga.[3]

### A realistic multi-season model

For a trader intending to join the documented June Kyiv convoy, the timing problem is especially clear.

- Season 1: leave the Mälaren region after northern waterways open; travel into the Rus’ network, trading or transferring cargo en route.
- Winter 1: remain at an established base or participate in regional activity. Which settlement, and on what contractual terms, is not predetermined.
- Season 2: reach or already be at Kyiv before the June departure; travel downstream and conduct business in Byzantium.
- Subsequent return: return to Rus’, with the further journey to Scandinavia depending on timing and transport choices.

This is a coherent reconstruction, not proof that every Birka merchant followed a two- or three-summer itinerary.

There is also an important legal constraint: the treaty of c. 944, preserved in the later *Primary Chronicle*, prohibits Rus’ wintering at St Mamas in Constantinople and at the Dnieper-mouth locations it names, directing them home to Rus’ in autumn. “Home” here need not mean Sweden. It reinforces the distinction between a Scandinavian visitor’s whole journey and a Rus’-based merchant’s annual southern circuit.[26][27]

### Why Baghdad needs separate treatment

Ibn Khordadbeh describes Rus’ merchants reaching the Caspian and sometimes carrying goods from Jurjan to Baghdad by camel, with interpreters. This supports occasional onward travel beyond the river-and-sea network—not a continuous “Birka-to-Baghdad sailing route,” and not a typical duration for it.[10]

Likewise, Islamic silver in Sweden is evidence of commercial connections, not proof that the person who brought a coin to Birka had visited its mint or Baghdad.

## 4. Winter hazards, prices, exactions, and lodging payments

### Disease: documented illness, not a documented winter epidemic

Ibn Fadlan reports that sick Rus’ were isolated in a tent with bread and water, with particularly harsh neglect of poor people and enslaved people. That is evidence for reported treatment of illness, not a diagnosis or a winter mortality rate. Montgomery also cautions that portions of Ibn Fadlan’s account depend on interpreters and information he could not have observed directly.[2]

Unknown: a securely identified outbreak of influenza, dysentery, scurvy, plague, or another named disease among Scandinavian traders wintering at one of the specified settlements in c. 850–970.

Crowding, cold, nutritional stress, and infection are reasonable potential hazards in a reconstruction. They should not be turned into a documented historical epidemic without additional evidence.

### Food supply and prices

Documented: Constantine’s Rus’ received winter maintenance from tributary populations. This is a provisioning arrangement, though not a market-price schedule.[1]

Unknown: reliable winter prices for bread, grain, meat, fuel, or lodging paid by Scandinavian visiting crews at Ladoga, Gnezdovo, Kyiv, Bulgar, or Itil during the requested period.

Ibn Fadlan does provide striking descriptions of cold and cheap firewood during his own prolonged winter stay in Khwarazm. But that was the caliphal embassy on a different route, not a Rus’ crew wintering on the Volga. Those figures cannot responsibly be used as a Bulgar or Ladoga budget.[28]

### Violence and insecurity

Constantine directly documents the threat of Pecheneg attacks during portages and along the lower Dnieper–Black Sea route. He describes guards being posted while goods, boats, and chained captives were moved past a major rapid. These are voyage hazards, not evidence of a winter attack on merchant quarters.[1]

For winter residence, dependence on hosts, rulers, and local protection is a plausible vulnerability. Yet a source describing general regional violence does not establish that foreign traders were routinely attacked each winter.

The later Novgorod agreements’ provisions for escorts, guarantees, and legal responsibility show how seriously merchant security could be organized. They remain later analogues.[17]

### Tribute, customs, and lodging must be kept separate

These were different obligations:

- Tribute: populations maintaining or supplying a ruling group, as in Constantine’s *poliudia*.
- Customs or commercial exactions: charges on arriving traders and merchandise.
- Lodging payment: a charge specifically for accommodation.

Ibn Fadlan reports a Bulgar royal claim to one enslaved person in ten when traders brought captives for sale. That is a commercial exaction, not a winter room charge.[29]

Likewise, the wider Arabic evidence for taxes and tribute along the Khazar and Bulgar networks should not be converted into evidence that merchants paid “winter rent” to a prince.[11][30]

### Is paying for winter lodging actually attested?

For Scandinavian/Rus’ visiting crews at these settlements in c. 850–970: no explicit winter lodging contract, quoted rent, or identifiable lodging payment was established by the evidence reviewed.

That does not mean accommodation was free. It means we cannot securely choose among cash rent, payment in goods, reciprocal hospitality, patronage, an existing household connection, communal ownership, or compulsory maintenance.

For the later medieval period, rented accommodation and regulated merchant establishments are documented and discussed in the Novgorod scholarship. They demonstrate possible institutional solutions, not their unchanged existence three centuries earlier.[17]

## Conclusion

The evidence supports winter residence as an integral part of the Rus’ trading world, but not a single standardized practice of “Vikings booking winter quarters at every trading post.”

The most secure reconstruction combines:

- resident communities at major nodes;
- visiting traders living ashore in communal accommodation;
- winter provisioning and tribute collection;
- changes of boat, equipment, and potentially transport mode;
- summer departures coordinated around regional waterways and convoy schedules.[1][2][3][5]

The principal limits are equally important. Archaeology rarely identifies a temporary winter visitor; Ibn Fadlan’s merchant houses are not explicitly winter quarters; boat burials are not winter storage; later Novgorod treaties cannot supply tenth-century lodging terms; and neither exact Birka-to-destination durations nor precise medieval ice calendars are presently established by the sources reviewed.
